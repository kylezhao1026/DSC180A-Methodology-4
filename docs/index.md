**What is the most interesting topic covered in your domain this quarter?**<br>
The most interesting topic for me was how to make an open-ended political forecast checkable. A question about budget reconciliation might ask what could stall the process. An agent can explain its reasoning in writing, and we can check some of its central predictions separately, such as whether the Senate passes a bill and whether it becomes law. I found it interesting that the choice of sub-questions shapes what we can actually evaluate. If they miss the main point of the written forecast, checking each one tells us very little about the forecast as a whole.

**Describe a potential investigation you would like to pursue for your Quarter 2 Project.**<br>
I’d investigate whether the sub-questions from our Q1 pilot actually help us evaluate written forecasts. I’d start with a small set of U.S. politics questions whose outcomes we can verify. For each question, I’d give agents the same historical evidence and ask for both a written forecast and probabilities for the individual outcomes. I’d then evaluate the reasoning using evidence that was public at the time and score the probabilities against what eventually happened. I’d look closely at cases where those two evaluations diverge, like when an agent gets the outcome right but gives a weak explanation.

**What is a potential change you’d make to the approach taken in your current Quarter 1 Project?**<br>
I’d verify the resolution sources earlier. In the initial pilot, 17 of 20 candidates made it to the human review packet, but getting that far didn’t establish that the named sources could settle every sub-question. I’d start by tracing a few candidates end to end, from what was public on the forecast date to the exact official record that would settle each outcome. That would give me concrete examples of what a resolvable question looks like before generating a larger batch.

**What other techniques would you be interested in using in your project?**<br>
I’d like to test the scoring rubric with pairs of responses that differ in one specific way. For example, both responses could give the same probabilities, but only one explains how Senate passage affects the chances of enactment. I’d have two reviewers score the responses independently and compare where they disagree. That would help me see whether the rubric rewards the reasoning I care about and which criteria need clearer definitions.
